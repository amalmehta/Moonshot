Section 04 · Flight software & AI

The software that flies the rocket

A booster falling at the speed of a jet airliner has about half a minute to find a ship the size of a football field, slow to walking pace and touch down. No human could fly that. This page builds the pieces from first principles: redundant computers, estimating where you are (Kalman filter), keeping the rocket upright (thrust-vector control), deciding where to go (landing guidance), and the reliability math that ties it together. Then it gives you a booster to land.

1. Flight software architecture

Every guided vehicle runs the same loop many times a second: sense (read sensors), navigate (estimate position, velocity and attitude), guide (decide the path to the target), control (turn that into engine gimbal angles, throttle, fin and thruster commands) and actuate. Engineers call the middle three "GNC". What makes a launch vehicle special is that the loop must keep working while parts of the computer fail, with no chance to stop and reboot.

What is public, and what is inferred

SpaceX publishes little about its avionics. The table separates what its engineers have said in public (Reddit "ask me anything" sessions in 2013, 2020 and 2021, conference talks, Lars Blackmore's papers) from what is general aerospace practice that we infer applies.

Why not radiation-hardened processors? Rad-hard chips are generations behind and cost orders of magnitude more. A cosmic ray that flips a bit (a "single-event upset") in one core makes that core's output differ from its twin, so the string goes quiet and the other strings carry on. As long as upsets are rare and independent, three cheap computers that check each other can be more reliable than one expensive one. The reliability section puts numbers on that and lets you inject the bit flip yourself.

2. Navigation: where am I? (the Kalman filter)

No sensor tells the truth. An accelerometer is smooth and fast but has a small bias: integrate it twice and the position error grows like \(\tfrac12 b t^2\). An altimeter or GPS has no drift but is noisy from one reading to the next. The Kalman filter combines the two optimally: it predicts with the IMU and corrects with each fix, weighting each by how much it can be trusted.

Derivation in one dimension

Start with a scalar. You believe the altitude is \(\hat z^-\) with variance \(P^-\); a sensor reads \(z\) with noise variance \(R\). Look for the best linear blend \(\hat z^+ = \hat z^- + K(z - \hat z^-)\). Its error is \(e^+ = (1-K)e^- + K v\), with \(e^-\) and the sensor noise \(v\) independent, so

$$ P^+ = (1-K)^2 P^- + K^2 R . $$

Setting \(dP^+/dK = 0\):

$$ K = \frac{P^-}{P^- + R}, \qquad P^+ = (1-K)P^- = \frac{P^- R}{P^- + R} \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad \frac{1}{P^+} = \frac{1}{P^-} + \frac{1}{R}. $$

Information adds. A precise sensor (small \(R\)) gets \(K \to 1\); a poor one gets \(K \to 0\). Between fixes the state is propagated with the IMU. For state \(\mathbf x = [z,\ v,\ b]^\mathsf{T}\) (altitude, vertical speed, accelerometer bias) and accelerometer reading \(a_m\):

$$ \text{predict:}\quad \hat{\mathbf x}^- = F\hat{\mathbf x} + B a_m,\quad P^- = F P F^\mathsf{T} + Q, \qquad F = \begin{bmatrix}1 & \Delta t & -\tfrac12\Delta t^2\\ 0 & 1 & -\Delta t\\ 0&0&1\end{bmatrix},\ B = \begin{bmatrix}\tfrac12\Delta t^2\\ \Delta t\\ 0\end{bmatrix} $$ $$ \text{update:}\quad K = P^- H^\mathsf{T}(H P^- H^\mathsf{T} + R)^{-1},\quad \hat{\mathbf x}^+ = \hat{\mathbf x}^- + K(z - H\hat{\mathbf x}^-),\quad P^+ = (I - KH)P^-,\qquad H = [1\ \ 0\ \ 0]. $$
Why the filter can estimate a bias it never measures directly

The bias enters the prediction through \(F\): a bias error makes the predicted altitude curve away from the fixes in a characteristic \(t^2\) way. The off-diagonal terms of \(P\) record that correlation, so the gain row for \(b\) is non-zero and each altitude innovation nudges the bias estimate. Observability, formally, is the rank of \([H;\,HF;\,HF^2]\), which is 3 here. Turn off "Estimate accelerometer bias" in the lab below to see the 2-state filter lag behind the truth.

For a scalar random walk \(x_{k+1} = x_k + w\) (variance \(Q\)) the covariance converges to the root of \(P^{-2} - QP^- - QR = 0\), whatever the starting \(P\). The validation page checks the iterated filter against that closed form.

3. Control: keeping a rocket upright

A rocket steered by gimballing its engine at the bottom is an inverted pendulum, like balancing a broom on your hand. Aerodynamic forces acting ahead of the centre of mass push it further over the more it tilts. Linearised about vertical:

$$ I\ddot\theta = M_\alpha\,\theta - T\ell\sin\delta + M_{gust} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \ddot\theta = a\,\theta - b\,\delta + d(t),\qquad a = \frac{M_\alpha}{I},\ b = \frac{T\ell}{I}. $$

With no control, \(\theta \propto e^{\sqrt a\,t}\): unstable. A PID law \(\delta = K_p\theta + K_i\!\int\!\theta\,dt + K_d\dot\theta\) gives the closed loop \(\ddot\theta + bK_d\dot\theta + (bK_p - a)\theta = 0\) (ignoring \(K_i\)), so we need \(bK_p > a\) for a restoring "spring" and \(K_d > 0\) for damping. The real limits are the gimbal angle (a few degrees on Merlin-class engines, est.), the actuator's speed and bandwidth, and the flexible body's bending modes, which the controller must not excite (see exercise 7 on aliasing).

\(\theta,\ \delta\)tilt from vertical; engine gimbal angle
\(T,\ \ell,\ I\)thrust; distance gimbal → centre of mass; pitch moment of inertia
\(M_\alpha\)destabilising aerodynamic moment per radian of tilt
\(K_p, K_i, K_d\)proportional, integral, derivative gains (radians of gimbal per radian of tilt)

Balance the booster

4. Guidance: getting to zero speed at zero height

Falcon 9 lands on one Merlin engine. Even at its minimum throttle (about 40%, est.) that engine out-lifts a nearly empty booster: thrust-to-weight is above 1. So the booster cannot hover. If it stops above the deck it starts climbing, and the engine can't be shut down and relit in time. It has to reach zero velocity at exactly zero altitude. This is the "hoverslam" or "suicide burn", and timing it is the guidance problem.

The one-dimensional answer

With constant thrust acceleration \(a_T\) and no drag, braking from speed \(v\) takes the height \(h = v^2 / \big(2(a_T - g)\big)\). Light too late and no throttle setting can stop you in time; light too early at full thrust and you stop high. Real guidance therefore plans the burn at part throttle, here about 62% of maximum, which leaves margin both ways: it can brake harder if the air was thinner than expected, or softer if it was thicker.

ZEM/ZEV optimal feedback guidance

For a point mass in uniform gravity, \(\dot{\mathbf r} = \mathbf v,\ \dot{\mathbf v} = \mathbf u + \mathbf g\), define what would happen if the engine did nothing from now on:

$$ \mathrm{ZEM} = \mathbf r_f - \left(\mathbf r + \mathbf v\,t_{go} + \tfrac12\mathbf g\,t_{go}^2\right), \qquad \mathrm{ZEV} = \mathbf v_f - \left(\mathbf v + \mathbf g\,t_{go}\right). $$

The control that reaches \((\mathbf r_f, \mathbf v_f)\) in time \(t_{go}\) with the least \(\int|\mathbf u|^2dt\) is

$$ \boxed{\ \mathbf u = \frac{6\,\mathrm{ZEM}}{t_{go}^2} - \frac{2\,\mathrm{ZEV}}{t_{go}}\ } $$
Derivation (Pontryagin, three lines)

Hamiltonian \(H = \tfrac12|\mathbf u|^2 + \boldsymbol\lambda_r\!\cdot\mathbf v + \boldsymbol\lambda_v\!\cdot(\mathbf u + \mathbf g)\). Costate equations: \(\dot{\boldsymbol\lambda}_r = 0,\ \dot{\boldsymbol\lambda}_v = -\boldsymbol\lambda_r\), so \(\boldsymbol\lambda_v\) is linear in time, and \(\partial H/\partial\mathbf u = 0\) gives \(\mathbf u = -\boldsymbol\lambda_v = \mathbf c_0 + \mathbf c_1 s\) with \(s\) the time from now.

Integrate over \(T = t_{go}\). The control must supply exactly the missing velocity and position: \(\mathbf c_0T + \mathbf c_1T^2/2 = \mathrm{ZEV}\) and \(\mathbf c_0T^2/2 + \mathbf c_1T^3/6 = \mathrm{ZEM}\). Solving gives \(\mathbf c_0 = 6\mathrm{ZEM}/T^2 - 2\mathrm{ZEV}/T\). Applying \(\mathbf c_0\) now and re-solving every cycle turns the open-loop optimum into a feedback law that corrects for disturbances.

Nice consequence: choose \(t_{go} = 2h/(|v_z| + v_f)\), the time a constant deceleration would take, and the vertical ZEM/ZEV command reduces exactly to \(a_z = (v_z^2 - v_f^2)/(2h) + g\), the suicide-burn law. ZEM/ZEV is its multi-dimensional, optimal generalisation, and steers sideways at the same time.

ZEM/ZEV has no idea that thrust is bounded between a minimum and a maximum, that the thrust direction is limited, or that the booster must stay above a glide-slope cone near the ground. Our simulator handles those by clipping, which works but isn't optimal. That gap is what convex optimisation fills.

Convex guidance (G-FOLD) and lossless convexification

The real problem: minimise propellant \(\int\|\mathbf T\|\,dt\) subject to the dynamics, \(\dot m = -\|\mathbf T\|/v_e\), a glide-slope cone, a pointing limit, and \(\rho_1 \le \|\mathbf T\| \le \rho_2\). The lower thrust bound makes the feasible set a hollow shell, which is non-convex: local solvers can stall or fail, which is unacceptable onboard. Açıkmeşe and Ploen (2007) and later Açıkmeşe, Carson and Blackmore (2013) showed a relaxation that is lossless: introduce a slack \(\Gamma\) with

$$ \|\mathbf T\| \le \Gamma, \qquad \rho_1 \le \Gamma \le \rho_2, $$

a convex second-order cone. They proved that the optimum of the relaxed problem satisfies \(\|\mathbf T\| = \Gamma\), so it is also feasible and optimal for the original problem. With a change of variables for the mass, the whole thing becomes a second-order cone program that interior-point methods solve to global optimality in a predictable, bounded number of iterations. That is what makes it certifiable for flight. JPL tested it as G-FOLD on Masten's Xombie rocket (2012–13). Blackmore (2016) writes that SpaceX uses CVXGEN (Mattingley & Boyd) to generate custom solver code that runs onboard "in a fraction of a second". This is optimisation, not machine learning. The current flight algorithms are not public in detail.

5. Landing simulator

2-D point mass (downrange \(x\), altitude \(z\)) starting at 6 km, falling at terminal velocity, mass ≈ 35 t. Modelled: one Merlin 1D (thrust varies with ambient pressure, minimum throttle 40% est.), a 12° thrust-tilt limit (est.), mass depletion, drag with \(C_D = 1\) (est.) in the US-1976 atmosphere, wind with gusts, grid-fin steering before ignition (lift up to 0.15 × drag, est.), noisy IMU / GPS / altimeter feeding a Kalman filter per axis, and guidance at 50 Hz. Physics step 10 ms. Every run is reproducible from its seed.

Land the booster

Success criteria (ours, est.). Touchdown vertical speed ≤ 4 m/s and horizontal speed ≤ 2 m/s: our assumption of what crushable landing legs absorb, since SpaceX doesn't publish limits. Miss distance ≤ 10 m: Blackmore's stated drone-ship accuracy target (the deck is roughly 90 × 50 m). The engine must not run dry, and the booster must not stop above the deck. Since minimum-throttle T/W > 1, the sim then shuts the engine down and the stage falls.
How the simulated guidance works (and where it departs from the real thing)

Before ignition the grid fins steer toward the ship with a ZEM/ZEV-style lateral command, limited to the lift they can make. Ignition happens when the thrust acceleration needed for a constant-deceleration stop, \((v^2 - v_f^2)/2h + g - \tfrac13 D/m\), reaches 62% of the engine's maximum. The \(\tfrac13\) is the average of drag \(\propto v^2\) over a burn whose speed falls linearly. During the burn the ZEM/ZEV law is flown twice in sequence: first to a "gate" 30 m above the deck, arriving at the speed from which a gentle 3 m/s² deceleration ends at 1 m/s; then (switching 0.3 s before the gate, since ZEM/ZEV commands grow like 1/t_go² close to any waypoint) to the deck itself, aimed 0.5 m "through" it so sensor noise can't make the stage stop just above. The estimated drag is subtracted from the command, the thrust vector is clipped to 12° from vertical, and the throttle is clipped to [40%, 100%].

Open loop replays the throttle history of a calm, noise-free, on-target landing from the same start, with no steering. It lands perfectly when reality matches the plan and drifts with the wind when it doesn't. That gap is the whole argument for feedback.

Left out: attitude dynamics (the booster is a point mass, so "tilt" is the thrust direction), the entry burn and supersonic aerodynamics (we start subsonic), plume–aero interaction, engine start transients, ship motion, leg dynamics and propellant slosh. Real guidance uses convex optimisation with proper constraints rather than our clipped ZEM/ZEV.

Monte Carlo: how robust is it?

One successful landing proves little. Run 200 landings with random wind (up to ± the maximum), gust strength, sensor noise (0.5–1.5× the setting), lateral offset and drift, and look at the spread. This is how GNC engineers argue a design is safe: thousands of dispersed runs, not one demo. Runs use a 20 ms physics step in a background thread.

6. Reliability: why three computers and nine engines

k-of-n systems

If each of \(n\) independent units works with probability \(R\), the number that work is binomial, so the probability that at least \(k\) work is

$$ R_{k/n} = \sum_{j=k}^{n} \binom{n}{j} R^{j}(1-R)^{n-j}. $$

Triple modular redundancy (TMR) is 2-of-3: \(R_{TMR} = R^3 + 3R^2(1-R) = 3R^2 - 2R^3\). For \(R\) near 1 the failure probability is \(\approx 3(1-R)^2\): it takes two independent failures to lose the system. But TMR is worse than a single unit when \(R < \tfrac12\), and for exponential lifetimes its mean time to failure is \(5/(6\lambda)\), shorter than one unit's \(1/\lambda\). Redundancy buys reliability for a mission of bounded length, not longevity. Real systems repair or reconfigure, for example by dropping a failed string so the remaining two cross-check.

Engine out. Falcon 9 can lose an engine during parts of ascent and still reach orbit: on CRS-1 (Oct 2012) one Merlin shut down and Dragon still reached the ISS, although the secondary payload ended up in a lower orbit than planned. Super Heavy's 33 Raptors are meant to tolerate several engine-outs. More engines means more failures, so the question is whether tolerance grows faster than the failure count.

The independence trap. These formulas assume failures are independent. An engine that fails explosively can take out its neighbours; a software bug runs identically on all three "redundant" computers. Common-cause failures usually dominate once you have redundancy, which is why engine bays are shielded and why identical software must be tested so heavily. Some programmes add dissimilar backup software for the same reason.

Fault injection: triple redundancy with dual-core cross-checks

Three strings each compute the next throttle command (a 16-bit word) on two cores. A string whose cores disagree stays silent. The actuator side takes the majority of the strings still talking. Inject faults and watch what survives.

Tip: flip a bit in one core, then flip the same bit in its twin.

7. AI and autonomy: what's actually public

"AI" gets attached to anything automatic. The autonomy that flies SpaceX vehicles, as far as the public record shows, is classical estimation, control and optimisation. It is predictable, analysable and testable, which is why it is used.

Autonomous landing public

Booster landings use onboard convex-optimisation guidance (lossless convexification, custom solvers generated by CVXGEN), plus the navigation and control loops above. Not machine learning. Every landing is computed onboard. Ground controllers can't fly it, and radio delays and dropouts wouldn't allow it anyway. Source: Blackmore 2016; Blackmore's 2017 Berkeley talk.

Dragon docking public

Crew and Cargo Dragon dock with the ISS autonomously, first on Demo-1 (March 2019), using relative-navigation sensors (lidar and imagers; Dragon 1's "DragonEye" sensor) and onboard guidance. Crews can monitor, pause, retreat or take over manually from the touchscreens. Source: NASA/press coverage of Demo-1.

Starlink collision avoidance public

Starlink satellites manoeuvre autonomously using tracking data on other objects, and SpaceX reports the counts to the FCC every six months: about 50,000 manoeuvres in Dec 2023–May 2024, 148,696 in Jun–Nov 2025 and 207,152 in Dec 2025–May 2026. SpaceX manoeuvres at a collision probability of about 1 in 1,000,000, roughly 100× more conservative than the common industry threshold. Source: SpaceX semi-annual reports to the FCC as reported by Space.com.

Autonomous flight safety public

The AFSS replaces the human range-safety officer's destruct button with onboard computers that compare the vehicle's own GPS/IMU state against pre-approved flight corridors. It was first used as the primary system on CRS-10 (Feb 2017). The logic is rule-based, not learned, and is approved by range-safety authorities.

Where machine learning plausibly fits industry practice / speculation

The following describes how aerospace and manufacturing companies commonly use ML. None of it is a claim about specific SpaceX systems.

  • Manufacturing inspection. Computer vision on welds, X-ray and CT images and composite layups to flag defects for a human to review.
  • Telemetry anomaly detection. Models trained on thousands of nominal test-stand firings and flights that flag channels behaving unusually, so engineers know where to look. The decision stays with people and certified limits.
  • Network operations. Large constellations need traffic routing, beam scheduling and spectrum management, where optimisation and learned predictors are natural tools.
  • Design exploration. Surrogate models that stand in for expensive CFD or structural simulations during trade studies.
Why flight-critical loops stay classical. Certification needs bounded, explainable behaviour: proofs of convergence (convex solvers), stability margins (control), consistency checks (Kalman innovations). A neural-network policy can perform well on average but is hard to bound in the tails, and tail behaviour is what a landing has to get right. Research on learned guidance exists (for example adaptive ZEM/ZEV tuned by reinforcement learning), but we know of no public evidence that it flies on operational launch vehicles.
