How a rocket engine makes thrust

A rocket is a machine that throws mass backwards very fast. Everything else (turbopumps, regenerative cooling, full-flow staged combustion) is engineering to throw more mass faster while weighing less. We build it up from Newton’s laws and check each model against real engines.

1. Where thrust comes from

Draw a control volume around the engine. Propellant enters with (almost) no momentum and leaves through the nozzle exit of area \(A_e\) at speed \(v_e\) and pressure \(p_e\), while the outside air pushes back with ambient pressure \(p_a\). Newton’s second law for the gas, plus the pressure imbalance across the exit plane, gives the thrust equation:

$$ F = \dot m\, v_e + (p_e - p_a)\,A_e $$

The first term is momentum thrust; the second is pressure thrust. Because \(p_a\) falls as the rocket climbs, the same engine makes more thrust in vacuum. It’s convenient to fold both terms into one effective exhaust velocity \(c = F/\dot m\), and to quote it divided by standard gravity as the specific impulse:

$$ I_{sp} = \frac{F}{\dot m\, g_0} = \frac{c}{g_0} \qquad [\text{seconds}] $$

Isp in seconds is “how many seconds one kilogram of propellant can produce one kilogram-force of thrust”. It’s the single most important number for an engine’s efficiency.

\(v_e\)exhaust speed at the exit plane (m/s)\(p_e, p_a\)exit and ambient pressure (Pa)
\(A_e\)nozzle exit area (m²)\(g_0\)9.80665 m/s², a unit convention, not local gravity

2. The rocket equation, and why it’s a tyrant

Integrate momentum conservation as the rocket sheds propellant (derivation in exercise 9) and you get Tsiolkovsky’s equation:

$$ \Delta v = I_{sp}\, g_0 \ln\frac{m_0}{m_f} $$

Δv grows only with the logarithm of the mass ratio. Reaching low Earth orbit needs about 9.4 km/s once gravity and drag losses are included (see Flight Physics). With kerolox (Isp ≈ 300 s) a single stage would need \(m_0/m_f = e^{9400/2942} ≈ 24\): 96 % of the liftoff mass would be propellant, leaving 4 % for tanks, engines and payload. That is why every orbital rocket stages, and why reusing those expensive structures (rather than making them lighter) was SpaceX’s big bet.

3. Propellants and combustion

Combustion converts chemical energy into hot, light gas. In the chamber the gas is nearly at rest at temperature \(T_c\) and pressure \(p_c\). How fast the nozzle can then make it go depends on \(T_c / \mathcal{M}\), temperature over molar mass: hot and light is fast. That’s why hydrogen (light products) has the best Isp, even though kerosene burns slightly hotter.

Why SpaceX moved from kerosene to methane. Kerosene leaves soot (coking) in pumps and cooling channels: bad for an engine that should fly 50+ times. Methane burns clean, can be made on Mars from CO₂ and water (Sabatier reaction \(\mathrm{CO_2 + 4H_2 \to CH_4 + 2H_2O}\)), has higher Isp, and stays liquid at nearly the same temperature as LOX (≈ 91–112 K), which simplifies common-bulkhead tanks and lets both propellants autogenously pressurize their tanks. The cost is volume: methalox is ~20 % less dense, so Starship’s tanks are huge.
Characteristic velocity c*: the combustion figure of merit

For choked flow through a throat of area \(A_t\), mass flow is set by chamber conditions alone: \(\dot m = p_c A_t / c^*\), where

$$ c^* = \frac{\sqrt{\gamma R T_c}}{\gamma \sqrt{\left(\frac{2}{\gamma+1}\right)^{\frac{\gamma+1}{\gamma-1}}}}, \qquad R = \frac{R_u}{\mathcal{M}} $$

\(c^*\) depends only on the chemistry (\(T_c, \mathcal M, \gamma\)), not on the nozzle. Test stands measure \(p_c\), \(A_t\) and \(\dot m\) to get an experimental \(c^*\); the ratio to theory (\(\eta_{c^*}\), typically 0.97–0.99) says how complete combustion is.

4. Nozzle theory: turning heat into speed

The de Laval nozzle converges to a throat and then diverges. For steady, adiabatic, frictionless (isentropic) flow of a perfect gas, conservation of mass and energy give the area–Mach relation:

$$ \frac{A}{A^*} = \frac{1}{M}\left[\frac{2}{\gamma+1}\left(1+\frac{\gamma-1}{2}M^2\right)\right]^{\frac{\gamma+1}{2(\gamma-1)}} $$

Subsonic flow speeds up in a converging duct; supersonic flow speeds up in a diverging one. The flow reaches exactly Mach 1 at the throat (\(A^*\)). Downstream, every area ratio \(\varepsilon = A_e/A_t\) fixes an exit Mach number, and with it the exit pressure:

$$ \frac{p_e}{p_c} = \left(1+\frac{\gamma-1}{2}M_e^2\right)^{-\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}} $$

Energy conservation then gives the exhaust speed, and the thrust coefficient \(C_F = F/(p_c A_t)\) packages the nozzle’s contribution:

$$ v_e = \sqrt{\frac{2\gamma}{\gamma-1}\,R T_c\left[1-\left(\frac{p_e}{p_c}\right)^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}\right]}, \qquad C_F = \sqrt{\frac{2\gamma^2}{\gamma-1}\left(\frac{2}{\gamma+1}\right)^{\frac{\gamma+1}{\gamma-1}}\left[1-\left(\frac{p_e}{p_c}\right)^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}\right]} + \frac{p_e - p_a}{p_c}\,\varepsilon $$

So \(I_{sp} = c^* C_F / g_0\): chemistry × nozzle. A bigger area ratio expands the gas further (more \(v_e\)) but at sea level an over-expanded nozzle (\(p_e \ll p_a\)) loses pressure thrust and eventually the flow separates from the wall, which is violent. So sea-level engines use \(\varepsilon\) ≈ 16–35 and vacuum engines 90–165+.

What the ideal model leaves out. Real exhaust is a reacting mixture whose γ and molar mass change as it expands (shifting equilibrium), the boundary layer adds friction and heat loss, and bell nozzles aren’t perfectly axial (divergence loss). We fold these into an overall efficiency of 0.97 and an effective γ and molar mass fitted to one engine per propellant (Merlin 1D, Raptor 2). Every other engine is then a genuine prediction. See Validation: MVac comes out within 1.4 %, Raptor 3 within 1 %, RVac within 4 %.

5. Engine cycles: who drives the pumps?

To push hundreds of kilograms per second into a chamber at 100–350 bar you need pumps, and pumps need power. Pump power is flow × pressure rise:

$$ P_{pump} = \frac{\dot m\, \Delta p}{\rho\, \eta_{pump}} $$

For a Raptor that’s tens of megawatts (exercise 6). The cycle is where that power comes from:

Gas generator: Merlin

A small side-burner runs fuel-rich to keep the turbine cool, spins the turbine, and its exhaust is dumped overboard (the dark plume beside a Falcon 9’s engines). It’s simple and robust, but the dumped gas contributes almost no thrust: ~2–4 % of the flow wasted, a few % of Isp.

Full-flow staged combustion: Raptor

All the fuel runs through a fuel-rich preburner and all the oxygen through an oxygen-rich one; each drives its own turbopump, then both hot gas streams meet in the main chamber. Nothing is dumped, turbines run cooler for the same power, and chamber pressure can be enormous (≈ 300–350 bar). The cost is complexity, and oxygen-rich hot gas, which needed new alloys (SpaceX “SX500”).

6. Throttling, gimballing, restarting

Throttle

Reducing propellant flow lowers \(p_c\) and thrust. Merlin throttles to roughly 40 % (est.) and Raptor similarly. Throttling matters for max-Q, for limiting acceleration, and above all for landing: a nearly empty booster is so light that even one engine at minimum throttle exceeds its weight, so it can’t hover and must time a “hoverslam” perfectly (see Flight Software).

Gimbal

Engines pivot a few degrees on hydraulic or electric actuators to steer (thrust-vector control). A rocket in powered flight is an inverted pendulum, unstable without active control. Starship also has cold-gas/hot-gas reaction control thrusters and flaps.

Ignite and re-ignite

Merlin lights with a squirt of TEA–TEB (triethylaluminium–triethylborane), which ignites on contact with air or LOX, so the number of restarts is limited by the TEA–TEB cartridge. Raptor uses spark (torch) ignition: in principle unlimited restarts, which matters for boostback, landing and refilling flights.
