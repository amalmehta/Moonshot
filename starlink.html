Communications

Starlink

Thousands of small satellites in low orbits act as one network: each one lights up a few patches of ground with steerable radio beams, and lasers pass traffic between them. Here is every piece of it, from the orbit geometry to the antenna physics to the latency budget, worked out from first principles and checked against FCC filings and SpaceX’s published numbers.

1. Constellation geometry: Walker shells

A single satellite 550 km up sees only a circle about 940 km in radius (worked out in §3) and crosses it in a few minutes. To give everyone a satellite overhead at all times you need many satellites, spread out evenly. The standard way to spread them is a Walker-delta pattern: put \(T\) satellites in \(P\) equally spaced orbital planes, all at the same altitude and inclination \(i\), with \(S = T/P\) satellites equally spaced in each plane.

Walker’s shorthand is \(i{:}\,T/P/F\). The integer \(F\in\{0,\dots,P-1\}\) staggers neighbouring planes so their satellites don’t all cross the equator at the same moment:

$$\Omega_j = \frac{2\pi j}{P},\qquad u_{jk}(0) = \frac{2\pi k}{S} + \frac{2\pi F j}{T},\qquad j = 0..P-1,\ k = 0..S-1.$$

Here \(\Omega_j\) is the right ascension of the ascending node (RAAN, where plane \(j\) crosses the equator going north) and \(u\) is the argument of latitude (the angle travelled from that node). The FCC’s 2021 authorisation for the first generation lists the shells exactly in these terms:

The phasing factor \(F\) isn’t in the filings; \(F = 1\) is an assumption. Period and drift are computed below.

Propagating a circular orbit

For a circular orbit the satellite just moves uniformly in \(u\) at the mean motion \(n = \sqrt{\mu/a^3}\), where \(a = R_\oplus + h\). Rotating the in-plane position by the inclination and then the RAAN gives the Earth-centred inertial position:

$$\mathbf r = a\begin{pmatrix}\cos\Omega\cos u - \sin\Omega\sin u\cos i\\ \sin\Omega\cos u + \cos\Omega\sin u\cos i\\ \sin u\sin i\end{pmatrix},\qquad u(t) = u_0 + \dot u\,t,\quad \Omega(t) = \Omega_0 + \dot\Omega\,t.$$

To get Earth-fixed coordinates (so cities stay put), subtract Earth’s rotation angle \(\theta = \omega_\oplus t\) from \(\Omega\). That is all the globe below does, 14,488 times per frame: three sines, three cosines and a few multiplies per satellite, written into one typed array.

Why the planes drift: J2 nodal precession

Earth is about 21 km fatter at the equator than at the poles. That bulge is the \(J_2\) term of the gravity field, and it is the largest perturbation in low orbit. Averaged over an orbit, the bulge pulls the satellite towards the equatorial plane, which is a torque perpendicular to the orbit’s angular momentum. Like a tilted spinning top, the orbit plane doesn’t flatten; it precesses about Earth’s axis:

$$\boxed{\dot\Omega = -\frac32\, n\, J_2\left(\frac{R_\oplus}{a}\right)^2\cos i}\qquad(e = 0).$$
Derivation of \(\dot\Omega\) from the J2 potential

The J2 part of Earth’s potential is \(\displaystyle R = -\frac{\mu J_2 R_\oplus^2}{2r^3}\left(3\sin^2\phi - 1\right)\), with \(\phi\) the latitude. On an orbit, \(\sin\phi = \sin i\sin u\). Average over one circular orbit (\(r = a\), \(\langle\sin^2 u\rangle = \tfrac12\)):

$$\bar R = -\frac{\mu J_2 R_\oplus^2}{2a^3}\left(\tfrac32\sin^2 i - 1\right) = \frac{\mu J_2 R_\oplus^2}{4a^3}\left(2 - 3\sin^2 i\right).$$

Lagrange’s planetary equation for the node is \(\displaystyle\dot\Omega = \frac{1}{na^2\sqrt{1-e^2}\,\sin i}\frac{\partial\bar R}{\partial i}\). Since \(\partial\bar R/\partial i = -\dfrac{3\mu J_2R_\oplus^2}{2a^3}\sin i\cos i\) and \(\mu = n^2a^3\):

$$\dot\Omega = \frac{1}{na^2\sin i}\left(-\frac{3n^2 J_2R_\oplus^2}{2}\sin i\cos i\right) = -\frac32 nJ_2\left(\frac{R_\oplus}{a}\right)^2\cos i.\ \blacksquare$$

The same averaging gives the drift of the argument of latitude, which the globe also uses: \(\dot u = n\left[1 + \tfrac34J_2(R_\oplus/a)^2\left(8\cos^2 i - 2\right)\right]\) for \(e = 0\).

At 550 km and 53° the planes regress by about 4.5° per day. Because every plane in a shell has the same \(a\) and \(i\), they all drift at the same rate, so the Walker pattern survives intact; it just rotates slowly as a whole. That is the main reason a shell uses one altitude and one inclination. Shells at different altitudes or inclinations drift apart from each other, which is fine because they’re coordinated independently.

\(R_\oplus\)Earth equatorial radius, 6,378.137 km
\(a,\ h\)orbit radius and altitude, \(a = R_\oplus + h\)
\(n\)mean motion \(\sqrt{\mu/a^3}\) (rad/s); period \(T = 2\pi/n\)
\(i\)inclination of the orbit plane to the equator

\(T/P/F\)total satellites / planes / inter-plane phasing integer

Why 53°, and why polar shells?

A satellite in an orbit of inclination \(i\) only ever flies over latitudes \(|\phi| \le i\). Worse for the low latitudes: it spends most of its time near the extremes, because that is where its ground track turns around. For a uniformly moving satellite the fraction of time spent at latitude \(\phi\) goes as \(1/\sqrt{\sin^2 i - \sin^2\phi}\), which piles up at \(\phi \to \pm i\). So 53° shells put the densest coverage around 45–55°, right over the United States, Europe and southern Canada, where most paying customers are. The 70° shell and the 97.6° polar shells fill in Alaska, Scandinavia, the poles and the oceans in between. The 97.6° inclination isn’t arbitrary: at 560 km it is sun-synchronous. With \(\cos i < 0\), \(\dot\Omega\) is positive and matches the Sun’s apparent motion of 360° per year (0.9856°/day), so the polar planes keep the same lighting all year (exercise 3).

Where the model departs from reality: real Starlink satellites ride slightly elliptical orbits, manoeuvre for collision avoidance and station-keeping, sit at slightly different altitudes within a shell, and many slots are empty or being filled at any moment. The globe shows the authorised design, not today’s satellites.

3. Coverage math

Draw the triangle formed by Earth’s centre, the user and the satellite. The user sees the satellite at elevation \(\varepsilon\) above the horizon, so the angle at the user is \(90^\circ + \varepsilon\). The law of sines gives the angle at the satellite (the nadir angle \(\eta\)), and the three angles add to 180°, which leaves the Earth central angle \(\lambda\) between the user and the point directly below the satellite:

$$\sin\eta = \frac{R_\oplus\cos\varepsilon}{R_\oplus + h},\qquad \lambda = 90^\circ - \varepsilon - \eta,\qquad d = \sqrt{(R_\oplus+h)^2 - R_\oplus^2\cos^2\varepsilon} - R_\oplus\sin\varepsilon.$$

The slant range \(d\) comes from the law of cosines on the same triangle. The footprint (all users who can see the satellite above \(\varepsilon\)) is a spherical cap of angular radius \(\lambda\), ground radius \(R_\oplus\lambda\) and area \(2\pi R_\oplus^2(1-\cos\lambda)\).

How many satellites does continuous coverage need? (rough lower bound)

One cap covers the fraction \(f = (1-\cos\lambda)/2\) of the sphere. Circles can’t tile a surface without overlapping. The best you can do is a honeycomb: each satellite “owns” the regular hexagon inscribed in its circle, which has \(\tfrac{3\sqrt3}{2\pi} \approx 0.827\) of the circle’s area. So

$$N_{\min} \approx \frac{1}{0.827\,f} = \frac{2}{0.827\,(1-\cos\lambda)}.$$

At 550 km and 25°, \(\lambda = 8.45^\circ\) and \(N_{\min} \approx 223\). Moving orbits can’t hold a static honeycomb, and inclined shells crowd satellites near their turning latitudes, so real constellations need several times this for single coverage (Walker’s and Rider’s “streets of coverage” analyses make this precise). Starlink flies thousands because it is limited by capacity (how many users each beam can serve), not by coverage. That is also why the minimum elevation matters so much: raising \(\varepsilon\) from 25° to 40° shrinks the footprint area by a factor of about 2.3.

4. Phased-array antennas

Both the satellite and the user terminal use flat phased arrays: hundreds to thousands of small antenna elements whose signals are added with controlled phase. Nothing moves; the beam is steered electronically in microseconds, so the user terminal can hop between satellites that cross its sky in a few minutes.

Take \(N\) identical elements on a line, spaced \(d\). A plane wave from angle \(\theta\) (measured from broadside, the perpendicular) reaches element \(m\) with an extra path \(m\,d\sin\theta\), i.e. a phase \(m\,kd\sin\theta\) with \(k = 2\pi/\lambda\). If we also apply a phase \(m\beta\) to element \(m\) and add everything up:

$$AF(\theta) = \sum_{m=0}^{N-1} e^{\,j m \psi},\quad \psi = kd\sin\theta + \beta\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad |AF| = \left|\frac{\sin(N\psi/2)}{\sin(\psi/2)}\right|.$$
Summing the geometric series

\(\sum_{m=0}^{N-1} x^m = \dfrac{1-x^N}{1-x}\) with \(x = e^{j\psi}\). Factor out half-angles: \(1 - e^{jN\psi} = -2j\,e^{jN\psi/2}\sin(N\psi/2)\) and \(1 - e^{j\psi} = -2j\,e^{j\psi/2}\sin(\psi/2)\). The phase factors have modulus 1, leaving \(|AF| = |\sin(N\psi/2)/\sin(\psi/2)|\). Its maximum is \(N\), at \(\psi = 0\); dividing by \(N\) gives the normalised pattern plotted below. The full antenna pattern is this array factor times the pattern of one element.

  • Steering. The peak is at \(\psi = 0\). To put it at \(\theta_0\), choose the progressive phase \(\beta = -kd\sin\theta_0\). Then \(\psi = kd(\sin\theta - \sin\theta_0)\).
  • Beamwidth. The first nulls are at \(\psi = \pm 2\pi/N\), and the half-power beamwidth is about \(\theta_{3\,\mathrm{dB}} \approx 0.886\,\lambda/(Nd\cos\theta_0)\) rad. Twice the elements gives half the beamwidth, and the beam fattens by \(1/\cos\theta_0\) as you scan, because the array looks shorter from off-axis.
  • Grating lobes. \(|AF|\) is periodic in \(\psi\) with period \(2\pi\). Extra full-height peaks appear wherever \(\sin\theta_m = \sin\theta_0 + m\lambda/d\) lands inside \([-1, 1]\). To keep them out while scanning to \(\theta_0\), you need \(d/\lambda < 1/(1 + |\sin\theta_0|)\). At \(d \le \lambda/2\) the array can scan anywhere with no grating lobes.
  • Scan loss. The projected aperture shrinks as \(\cos\theta_0\), so gain drops by about \(10\log_{10}\cos\theta_0\) dB (3 dB at 60°), and more in practice because each element’s own pattern also falls off. That is why the scheduler prefers high-elevation satellites, and why the user terminal is tilted.

Polar plot: radius is the normalised pattern in dB (0 to −40 dB), broadside is straight up. Push the spacing past λ/2 and steer hard to watch a grating lobe appear.

In two dimensions the same rules apply along both axes. A square array of \(N_{el}\) elements at \(\lambda/2\) spacing has aperture \(A = N_{el}\lambda^2/4\) and broadside directivity \(4\pi A/\lambda^2 = \pi N_{el}\). The Standard user terminal’s roughly 0.59 m × 0.38 m face est. at 11.7 GHz (\(\lambda = 2.56\) cm) could hold about 1,400 elements at \(\lambda/2\), giving about 36 dBi before losses, or about 34.5 dBi at 65% efficiency.

5. Link budget

A link budget follows the power from transmitter to receiver. The satellite radiates an equivalent isotropic power \(\text{EIRP} = P_tG_t\). At distance \(d\) it has spread over \(4\pi d^2\), and an antenna of gain \(G_r\) collects an effective area \(G_r\lambda^2/4\pi\). The ratio is the free-space path loss:

$$\text{FSPL} = \left(\frac{4\pi d}{\lambda}\right)^2 = \left(\frac{4\pi d f}{c}\right)^2.$$

The receiver adds thermal noise of spectral density \(N_0 = kT_{sys}\). In decibels, the carrier-to-noise-density ratio and the signal-to-noise ratio in bandwidth \(B\) are

$$\frac{C}{N_0}\Big|_{\mathrm{dB\,Hz}} = \text{EIRP} - \text{FSPL} - L + \frac{G}{T} - 10\log_{10}k,\qquad \text{SNR} = \frac{C}{N_0} - 10\log_{10}B,\qquad C_{\text{Shannon}} = B\log_2(1 + \text{SNR}).$$

Here \(10\log_{10}k = -228.6\) dBW/(K·Hz), \(L\) lumps atmospheric, pointing and polarisation losses, and \(G/T\) (the receive gain over system noise temperature) is the single figure of merit of a receiving terminal. At capacity, \(E_b/N_0\) equals the Shannon limit \((2^\eta-1)/\eta\) at spectral efficiency \(\eta = R/B\); a real modem with coding needs a few dB more.

Where the defaults come from. SpaceX doesn’t publish per-beam EIRP or terminal G/T, so the defaults are estimates (est.): EIRP is back-solved from the ITU power-flux-density mask in 10.7–11.7 GHz (−140 dB(W/m²) in any 4 kHz for arrival angles above 25°, ITU RR Art. 21) at 550 km over 240 MHz; G/T ≈ 34.5 dBi (aperture estimate in §4) minus 10 log 180 K, less about 1.5 dB of scan loss. The Ku-band user downlink is 10.7–12.7 GHz in the FCC filings; 240 MHz is about one downlink channel est..

6. Laser links and latency

Light in glass fibre travels at \(c/n\) with group index \(n \approx 1.47\), about 32% slower than in vacuum. Satellites linked by lasers carry light at the full \(c\). So for long distances a path that climbs 550 km, runs along the shell and comes back down can beat even a perfectly straight fibre. Ignoring the minimum elevation, the up-over-down path wins once

$$\frac{2h + (R_\oplus + h)\theta}{c} < \frac{n R_\oplus\theta}{c}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \theta > \frac{2h}{R_\oplus(n-1) - h},$$

which is about 2,870 km of ground distance at 550 km. With 25° slant legs (each one already covers 940 km of ground horizontally), the bound is better still. Real fibre routes are longer than great circles, so the advantage grows. Each Starlink satellite since v1.5 carries laser terminals (SpaceX says three per V2 mini est.). The page routes London → New York on a “+Grid” topology: every satellite in the 550 km / 53° shell links to its two in-plane neighbours and to the same slot in each adjacent plane. The page then finds the shortest path with Dijkstra’s algorithm, from a satellite the London user can see to one the New York user can see.

Comparing like with like

SpaceX’s own latency explainer quotes the speed-of-light part as 1.8–3.6 ms per ground↔satellite leg and “usually under 10 ms for the round trip”. Our geometry gives 1.83 ms straight up and 3.75 ms at the 25° edge (see the Validation page). The measured median round trip is much larger, 25.7 ms in the US at peak hours as of mid-2025, because it also includes radio scheduling on the shared beam, processing, gateway-to-internet backhaul and queueing. Propagation is a floor, not a prediction: what our model can say is that about 60–70% of today’s median is not physics but engineering, which is why SpaceX was able to cut its median from 48.5 ms to 33 ms in a few months of 2024 mostly with software.

7. Satellite bus basics and economics

A V2 mini weighs about 800 kg at launch (SpaceX/Spaceflight Now, 2023) and is a flat panel designed to stack tightly in a Falcon 9 fairing, with two large solar arrays, phased arrays on the Earth-facing side, laser terminals and an electric propulsion system. The first-generation satellites used krypton Hall thrusters. The V2 mini’s argon Hall thruster was developed in-house and has 2.4× the thrust and 1.5× the specific impulse of the krypton unit: about 170 mN and \(I_{sp}\approx 2{,}500\) s est., so krypton is roughly 1,500–1,700 s est.. Argon is far cheaper than xenon, which matters when you’re buying propellant for thousands of satellites.

Falcon 9 drops the stack in a low orbit (≈280–350 km est.). Each satellite checks itself out there and then spirals up on electric thrust. A satellite that fails checkout is already low enough to re-enter on its own. For a slow, tangential, coplanar spiral, every orbit along the way is nearly circular, and the thrust exactly cancels the speed change between them:

$$\Delta v_{\text{spiral}} \approx |v_1 - v_2| = \left|\sqrt{\mu/r_1} - \sqrt{\mu/r_2}\right|,\qquad m_p = m_0\left(1 - e^{-\Delta v/(I_{sp}g_0)}\right),\qquad t \approx \frac{m\,\Delta v}{F}.$$

Once up, the satellite still fights drag. For a circular orbit, drag \(\tfrac12\rho v^2C_DA\) removes orbital energy \(-\mu m/2a\), which gives the decay rate

$$\frac{da}{dt} = -\frac{\rho\sqrt{\mu a}}{B},\qquad B = \frac{m}{C_D A}.$$

Thermospheric density \(\rho\) falls by roughly a factor of 30 between 300 and 550 km, and it swings by an order of magnitude with the 11-year solar cycle. That is the safety story of low orbits: a dead satellite at 550 km comes down within a few years, one at 300 km within days to weeks. In February 2022 a geomagnetic storm raised the density at the ~210 km insertion orbit enough to bring down most of a batch of 49 satellites.

Satellites per launch

Falcon 9 carries 21–29 V2 mini satellites per launch, depending on version and landing mode (Wikipedia’s launch list). That number is mostly mass: a reusable Falcon 9 lifts on the order of 17 t est. to the low insertion orbit, and \(17{,}000/800 \approx 21\). Lighter variants and lower insertion squeeze in more. At Falcon 9’s published list price of about \$70 M, a launch of 24 satellites would cost about \$2.9 M per satellite. SpaceX launches its own satellites on reused boosters at an internal cost well below list (see the Cost page), which is what makes refreshing a constellation of satellites with ~5-year lives affordable.
