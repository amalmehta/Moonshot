Section 02 · Flight physics

From the pad to orbit

Space is only ~100 km up, an hour’s drive. Orbit is hard because you have to go 7.8 km/s sideways and pay a toll to gravity and air on the way. This page simulates a real ascent and shows exactly where the Δv goes.

1. The atmosphere we fly through

Hydrostatic balance (\(dp/dz = -\rho g\)) plus the ideal-gas law (\(p = \rho R T\)) give pressure that falls roughly exponentially with altitude, with scale height \(H = RT/g ≈ 7\) km. The U.S. Standard Atmosphere 1976 refines this with seven layers of piecewise-linear temperature:

$$ p(h) = \begin{cases} p_b\left(\dfrac{T_b}{T_b + L_b (h - h_b)}\right)^{g_0/(R L_b)} & L_b \ne 0 \\[2mm] p_b \exp\!\left(-\dfrac{g_0 (h-h_b)}{R T_b}\right) & L_b = 0 \end{cases} $$

where \(h\) is geopotential altitude and \(L_b\) the layer’s lapse rate. The model on this site implements exactly that and matches the published tables to better than 0.2 % (see Validation).

2. Orbit is going sideways fast enough to keep missing the ground

Newton’s cannonball: fire horizontally fast enough and the ground curves away as fast as you fall. For a circular orbit, gravity is exactly the centripetal force:

$$ \frac{\mu}{r^2} = \frac{v^2}{r} \;\Rightarrow\; v_{circ} = \sqrt{\frac{\mu}{r}} ≈ 7.78\ \text{km/s at 200 km} $$

Climbing 200 km costs comparatively little energy (\(g h\) ≈ 2 MJ/kg vs \(v^2/2\) ≈ 30 MJ/kg for the speed). But the rocket must first get out of the thick lower atmosphere, and every second it spends thrusting against gravity is wasted. The compromise is the gravity turn: rise vertically for a few seconds, tip over a few degrees (the “pitch kick”), then let gravity bend the trajectory while the engines push along the velocity vector, so there’s no angle of attack and no sideways aerodynamic load.

A 2-D point-mass simulation in an inertial frame: inverse-square gravity, the US-1976 atmosphere rotating with Earth, drag \(D = q C_D A\), Isp and thrust that change with ambient pressure, a gravity turn for stage 1, a max-Q throttle bucket, and a closed-loop guidance law for stage 2 (zero-effort-miss/zero-effort-velocity in the radial direction, \(a_r = 6\,\mathrm{ZEM}/t_{go}^2 - 2\,\mathrm{ZEV}/t_{go}\)) that drives altitude to the target and radial speed to zero. 4. Where the Δv goes

Project the equation of motion onto the velocity direction and integrate. The Δv the engines produce (\(\int F/m\,dt\)) splits into what you actually gain plus three losses:

$$ \underbrace{\int \frac{F}{m}\,dt}_{\text{rocket equation}} = \Delta v_{gained} + \underbrace{\int g \sin\gamma\, dt}_{\text{gravity}} + \underbrace{\int \frac{D}{m}\, dt}_{\text{drag}} + \underbrace{\int \frac{F}{m}(1-\cos\alpha)\, dt}_{\text{steering}} $$

\(\gamma\) is the flight-path angle above horizontal and \(\alpha\) the angle between thrust and velocity. Gravity loss dominates: a big rocket is heavy and accelerates slowly at first. Drag loss is surprisingly small for big rockets (the square-cube law: mass grows faster than frontal area). Steering loss is what upper-stage guidance spends pointing thrust partly upward to hold altitude while it builds horizontal speed.

Run the simulator to see this flight’s loss budget.

5. Staging: throwing away dead weight

Give each of \(N\) stages the same Isp and structural coefficient \(\varepsilon = m_{dry}/(m_{dry}+m_{prop})\), and split Δv equally (optimal in that case). Each stage’s mass ratio is \(R = e^{\Delta v/(N I_{sp} g_0)}\), and the payload fraction is

$$ \pi = \left(\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}\right)^N, \qquad \lambda = \frac{1-\varepsilon R}{R-1}. $$

If \(\varepsilon R \ge 1\) a stage cannot even carry its own structure: the mission is impossible with that many stages.

How many stages?

The reuse twist. Recovering a booster at sea means keeping about 8 % of its propellant for the entry and landing burns (more if it also has to boost back), plus legs and grid fins: payload drops ~20–25 % (set the reserve slider above to 8 %). SpaceX’s bet is that flying the same booster 20–30 times saves far more money than the lost payload costs. See Cost.

6. Orbital mechanics

Energy conservation per unit mass, \(\tfrac12 v^2 - \mu/r = -\mu/(2a)\), gives the vis-viva equation, the workhorse of mission design:

$$ v^2 = \mu\left(\frac{2}{r} - \frac{1}{a}\right), \qquad T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{a^3}{\mu}} $$

The cheapest two-impulse transfer between circular orbits is the Hohmann transfer: an ellipse tangent to both, with burns at perigee and apogee.
