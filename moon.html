Moon Mission

Send me to the Moon. Here a numerical integrator flies a lander from Earth orbit to the Moon's surface: it aims the trans-lunar burn, coasts three days through the pull of two bodies, brakes into lunar orbit and lands. A planner then counts how many tanker flights it takes to fill the tanks for the trip.

    Mission planner

    The lander is modelled on a Starship-style human landing system: one big ship that launches nearly empty to low Earth orbit (LEO), is filled up there by tanker flights, then flies to the Moon and lands. Change the inputs and the planner re-solves the trajectory (in a background thread) and re-does the propellant accounting.

    Simplified on purpose. NASA's Artemis plan is different: the crew launch on SLS in Orion, the lander waits in a near-rectilinear halo orbit (NRHO, a 6.5-day orbit that swings from ~3,000 to ~70,000 km above the Moon), crew transfer there, and the lander also carries them back up to NRHO. Propellant is first gathered in a depot ship. This page flies the textbook profile instead: LEO → trans-lunar injection → a 100 km circular low lunar orbit (LLO) → surface, with tankers filling the lander directly, and (optionally) an ascent back to LLO where a crew vehicle would pick you up. Masses marked est. are estimates, not published SpaceX figures.

    Vis-viva: speed from where you are

    Almost every number on this page comes from one equation. For a body moving under a single inverse-square attractor, the specific orbital energy (kinetic plus potential, per kilogram) is constant along the orbit and depends only on the semi-major axis:

    $$ \varepsilon = \frac{v^2}{2} - \frac{\mu}{r} = -\frac{\mu}{2a} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad v^2 = \mu\left(\frac{2}{r} - \frac{1}{a}\right). $$
    Why ε = −μ/2a

    At periapsis and apoapsis the velocity is perpendicular to the radius, so angular momentum \(h = r_p v_p = r_a v_a\). Write energy conservation between the two: \(\tfrac12 v_p^2 - \mu/r_p = \tfrac12 v_a^2 - \mu/r_a\). Substitute \(v_a = v_p r_p/r_a\) and solve: \(v_p^2 = 2\mu\, r_a / (r_p(r_p+r_a))\). Then \(\varepsilon = \tfrac12 v_p^2 - \mu/r_p = \mu\left(\frac{r_a}{r_p(r_p+r_a)} - \frac{1}{r_p}\right) = -\frac{\mu}{r_p + r_a} = -\frac{\mu}{2a}\).

    Two special cases follow at once: a circular orbit (\(a = r\)) has \(v_c = \sqrt{\mu/r}\), and escape (\(a \to \infty\)) needs \(v_\text{esc} = \sqrt{2\mu/r} = \sqrt2\,v_c\).

    SymbolMeaningValue used
    \(\mu_\mathrm{E},\ \mu_\mathrm{M}\)Gravitational parameter \(GM\) of Earth, Moon3.986 × 10¹⁴, 4.905 × 10¹² m³/s²

    Why trans-lunar injection is about 3.1 km/s

    Pretend the Moon is massless and just ask what burn in a circular parking orbit of radius \(r_1\) raises apogee to the Moon's distance \(r_2\). That is the first half of a Hohmann transfer: an ellipse with \(a = (r_1+r_2)/2\), entered with a single tangential burn,

    $$ \Delta v_\text{TLI} = \sqrt{\frac{\mu_\mathrm{E}}{r_1}}\left(\sqrt{\frac{2 r_2}{r_1 + r_2}} - 1\right) \approx 3.13\ \text{km/s} \quad (r_1 = R_\mathrm{E} + 200\ \text{km}). $$

    The number is so stable because \(r_2 \gg r_1\): the square root is already \(\sqrt{2}\) to within 2 %, so TLI is “nearly escape”: the perigee speed after the burn, 10.92 km/s, is just 93 m/s short of escape speed at that height. The minimum-energy ellipse takes \(\pi\sqrt{a^3/\mu_\mathrm{E}} \approx 5\) days; burning a few tens of m/s more cuts that to about 3 days, because a minimum-energy ship crawls near apogee. The planner's integrated answer, …, sits a little above the Hohmann value for exactly this reason.

    Patched conics and the sphere of influence

    The classic hand method splits the trip in two. Far from the Moon, treat the ship as orbiting Earth alone; close to it, as orbiting the Moon alone; and stitch (“patch”) the two conics together at the boundary of the Moon's sphere of influence (SOI). Laplace defined that boundary as the distance where it becomes equally accurate to use either body as the centre.

    Derivation: rSOI = a (m/M)2/5

    Let the Moon (mass \(m\)) be at distance \(a\) from Earth (mass \(M\)) and the ship at a small distance \(r\) from the Moon.

    Moon-centred view. Main acceleration \(Gm/r^2\). Earth's effect in this (accelerating) frame is only its tidal part: the difference between Earth's pull on the ship and on the Moon, \(\approx GM r/a^3\) for \(r \ll a\). Ratio: \(\dfrac{GMr/a^3}{Gm/r^2} = \dfrac{M}{m}\left(\dfrac{r}{a}\right)^3\).

    Earth-centred view. Main acceleration \(\approx GM/a^2\); the Moon perturbs it by \(\approx Gm/r^2\) (its pull on Earth, \(Gm/a^2\), is negligible next to that). Ratio: \(\dfrac{Gm/r^2}{GM/a^2} = \dfrac{m}{M}\left(\dfrac{a}{r}\right)^2\).

    Set the ratios equal: \(\dfrac{M}{m}\left(\dfrac{r}{a}\right)^3 = \dfrac{m}{M}\left(\dfrac{a}{r}\right)^2 \Rightarrow \left(\dfrac{r}{a}\right)^5 = \left(\dfrac{m}{M}\right)^2\), so

    $$ r_\text{SOI} = a\left(\frac{m}{M}\right)^{2/5} = 384{,}400\ \text{km}\times 0.0123^{0.4} \approx 66{,}200\ \text{km}. $$

    Inside the SOI the ship is on a hyperbola about the Moon. Matching velocities at the boundary gives the arrival speed relative to the Moon, \(v_\infty\) (the Moon moves at 1.02 km/s; a Hohmann ship at apogee only at 0.19 km/s, so \(v_\infty \approx 0.84\) km/s), and energy on the hyperbola gives the speed at periselene and the lunar orbit insertion (LOI) burn to circularize there:

    $$ v_p = \sqrt{v_\infty^2 + \frac{2\mu_\mathrm{M}}{r_p}}, \qquad \Delta v_\text{LOI} = v_p - \sqrt{\frac{\mu_\mathrm{M}}{r_p}}. $$

    Patched conics get within a few percent of the real numbers (see the comparison), which is why they are still taught and still used for first guesses. But they cannot tell you which lead angle actually hits a 100 km periselene, because that depends on how the Moon's pull bends the path over the last day, and errors of 1 m/s at TLI become hundreds of km at the Moon.

    Three bodies: why we integrate

    Two bodies have a closed-form answer (conic sections). Three do not: Poincaré showed in the 1890s that the general three-body problem has no solution in terms of ordinary functions, and nearby trajectories can diverge chaotically. So we write down Newton's law and integrate it numerically. In a frame centred on Earth, with the Moon at \(\mathbf r_\mathrm{M}(t)\):

    $$ \ddot{\mathbf r} = -\mu_\mathrm{E}\frac{\mathbf r}{|\mathbf r|^3} - \mu_\mathrm{M}\left(\frac{\mathbf r - \mathbf r_\mathrm{M}}{|\mathbf r - \mathbf r_\mathrm{M}|^3} + \frac{\mathbf r_\mathrm{M}}{|\mathbf r_\mathrm{M}|^3}\right). $$

    The last term is the indirect term. Earth-centred coordinates are not inertial: the Moon accelerates Earth toward itself by \(\mu_\mathrm{M} \mathbf r_\mathrm{M}/|\mathbf r_\mathrm{M}|^3\), so we subtract that from the ship's acceleration. Leave it out and a ship near Earth would feel the Moon's full pull instead of only the much smaller tidal difference. The Moon itself moves on a circle at \(n = \sqrt{(\mu_\mathrm{E}+\mu_\mathrm{M})/a_\mathrm{M}^3}\), a 27.3-day sidereal month.

    The integrator is the Dormand–Prince Runge–Kutta 5(4) pair (the method behind MATLAB's ode45). Each step evaluates the acceleration seven times and forms two estimates of the new state, one 5th-order and one 4th-order accurate. Their difference estimates the error; the step is accepted if that error is below a tolerance (here \(10^{-10}\) relative) and the next step size is scaled by \(0.9\,(\text{tol}/\text{err})^{1/5}\). Near Earth the steps are seconds long; in mid-coast they stretch to hours. Closest approach to the Moon is found as a root of \((\mathbf r - \mathbf r_\mathrm{M})\cdot(\mathbf v - \mathbf v_\mathrm{M}) = 0\) by bisection inside the step.

    How the planner aims

    The TLI burn is a tangential impulse at a fixed point of the parking orbit. There are two unknowns, the burn size \(\Delta v\) and the Moon's lead angle \(\lambda\), and two conditions. The first is that periselene lands at the target altitude on the far side, passing the Moon clockwise (retrograde, as Apollo did). The second is a free return: if the LOI burn never happens, the Moon's gravity swings the ship back to Earth with a 60 km perigee, ready for re-entry. That is how Apollo 8, 10 and 11 flew (Apollo 13 depended on it). The solver is two nested 1-D root finds. For each trial \(\Delta v\), a coarse scan in \(\lambda\) brackets the angle that gives the target periselene and a regula-falsi search refines it. An outer search on \(\Delta v\) then drives the return perigee to 60 km. That is a few hundred integrations, about half a second, run in a Web Worker.

    The Oberth effect: burn where you are fast

    Why burn for the Moon from low orbit instead of climbing first? A burn changes kinetic energy by \(\Delta(\tfrac12 v^2) = v\,\Delta v + \tfrac12\Delta v^2\): the same \(\Delta v\) buys more energy when \(v\) is already high, and the ship is fastest at the bottom of the gravity well. After a burn at speed \(v_0\) where the escape speed is \(v_\text{esc}\), conservation of energy gives the speed left over far away:

    $$ v_\infty = \sqrt{(v_0 + \Delta v)^2 - v_\text{esc}^2}. $$

    From a 200 km orbit (\(v_0 = 7.78\), \(v_\text{esc} = 11.01\) km/s), 3.22 km/s just escapes and 3.40 km/s leaves with \(v_\infty = 1.97\) km/s: the last 175 m/s bought almost 2 km/s. The propellant's own kinetic energy is what pays for this. Exhaust dumped deep in the well leaves with less energy, so more of it ends up in the ship.

    Lunar orbit insertion, descent and ascent

    At periselene the ship is moving at about 2.5 km/s relative to the Moon, faster than the Moon's 2.3 km/s escape speed at 100 km, so without a burn it leaves again (the free return). A retrograde LOI burn of about 0.9 km/s drops it to the 1.63 km/s circular speed.

    Deorbit. A small Hohmann-style burn (≈ 20 m/s) lowers the far side of the orbit from 100 km to 15 km, where powered descent begins (Apollo's “PDI” altitude, chosen so the LM was never on a surface-intersecting path until it lit the engine).

    Powered descent. The model flies a gravity turn: constant thrust, always pointing straight against the velocity. With flight-path angle \(\gamma\) (negative going down) and local gravity \(g = \mu_\mathrm{M}/r^2\),

    $$ \dot v = -\frac{T}{m} - g\sin\gamma, \qquad v\,\dot\gamma = -\left(g - \frac{v^2}{r}\right)\cos\gamma, \qquad \dot m = -\frac{T}{I_{sp} g_0}. $$

    One number is free, the thrust level, and it is solved by bisection so that the speed reaches zero exactly at the surface (too little thrust and you hit the ground fast; too much and you stop high). The Δv is \(I_{sp} g_0 \ln(m_0/m_f)\). It exceeds the starting speed only by the gravity loss \(\int g\sin|\gamma|\,dt\), which is small for a short, punchy burn. Real landers spend more: Apollo's LM had a lunar thrust-to-weight of only about 1.8, held altitude while braking, pitched up so the crew could see the site, and carried a hover reserve.

    Ascent. The reverse: a 10 s vertical rise, a small pitch-over kick, then a gravity turn with thrust along the velocity at lunar T/W = 2. The kick is solved so the engine cuts off flying level just as the coasting orbit's high point reaches the target. A last small burn circularizes there. The loss-free minimum (a Hohmann hop from the surface) is 1.73 km/s; the integrated ascent costs about 1.9 km/s.

    Why refill: the tyranny of the rocket equation

    The rocket equation \(\Delta v = I_{sp} g_0 \ln(m_0/m_f)\) is exponential in the wrong direction. Add up the Δv after low Earth orbit (TLI, corrections, LOI, deorbit, descent, hover, and ascent if you want to leave again): about 7.9 km/s. With \(v_e = 370 \times 9.81 = 3.63\) km/s the required mass ratio is ≈ 8.9. A lander weighing 140 t at the end therefore needs ≈ 1,070 t of propellant in LEO.

    But a ship that has just flown to orbit arrives with only the propellant its own climb didn't use. The planner estimates ≈ 130 t (booster burn-out with a 10 % landing reserve, est.; the ship then burns what the remaining ~9.4 km/s to LEO needs). Launching fully fuelled is impossible: the ship would be too heavy to reach orbit. So the tanks are filled in orbit, one tanker at a time. The planner counts flights given each tanker's delivered propellant. Public estimates for a real HLS mission range from under ten to about twenty flights, and SpaceX hasn't published a firm number (est.).

    Boil-off

    Liquid oxygen (−183 °C) and methane (−162 °C) slowly boil as heat leaks in through insulation, plumbing and sunlight. A heat leak \(\dot Q\) evaporates \(\dot m = \dot Q / h_\text{vap}\) of propellant: with \(h_\text{vap} \approx 213\) kJ/kg for oxygen, a 1 kW leak boils 400 kg of LOX a day. The planner treats boil-off as a fixed fraction of the propellant aboard per day, so what was delivered first has been boiling longest. A slow tanker cadence and a leaky tank can add whole flights. The same loss applies on the way to the Moon and while waiting in lunar orbit or on the surface.
