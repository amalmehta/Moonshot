Section 06 · Cost

What it costs to fly

Rockets were expensive because we threw them away. A Falcon 9’s propellant costs about as much as a nice house; the hardware costs as much as an airliner. SpaceX’s whole strategy follows from that one fact. Every dollar figure below is an estimate (SpaceX publishes prices, not costs), and every slider is yours to disagree with.

1. Price per kilogram, through history

Harry Jones (NASA Ames, 2018) normalized launch costs to FY2018 dollars per kilogram to LEO. The Space Shuttle, partially reusable but needing an army to refurbish it, came in at ~$54,500/kg. Falcon 9 dropped that ~20×, and Falcon Heavy ~40×.

2. A Falcon 9 cost model

Split a launch into what you build, what you burn, and what you do. With a reusable booster the booster’s cost is amortized over its flights:

$$ C_{flight} = \frac{C_{booster}}{N} + C_{refurb} + C_{recovery} + C_{2nd\,stage} + \frac{C_{fairing}}{N_f} + C_{prop} + C_{ops} $$

Price, for comparison: ~$74 M (2026 list, press reports). The gap between cost and price funds development (Starship, Starlink), failures and profit.

3. Propellant is (almost) free

Falcon 9 burns ~488 t of propellant: ~343 t of liquid oxygen (made from air, roughly $0.10–0.20/kg in bulk) and ~145 t of RP-1 (refined kerosene, ~$1/kg). That’s about $200k, matching Musk’s 2011 estimate and well under 1 % of the vehicle’s cost. A full Starship stack burns ~4,900 t of methalox, about $0.9 M. If the hardware lasts, propellant sets the floor: under $10/kg.

4. How many flights does a booster need?

Amortization has diminishing returns: going from 1 to 5 flights cuts the booster’s share by 80 %; going from 20 to 40 saves another $0.75 M. Past ~10 flights, the second stage and operations dominate.

5. Starship: reusing everything

Starship’s design goal is full and rapid reuse: booster caught by the tower within minutes, ship caught later, no expendable stage at all. Its build costs are unknown publicly. 6. Learning curves and vertical integration

T. P. Wright (1936) observed that every doubling of cumulative aircraft production cut unit labour cost by a roughly constant fraction \(b\):

$$ C_n = C_1\, n^{\log_2 b} $$

With \(b = 0.85\), the 100th unit costs 34 % of the first. SpaceX builds Merlins by the hundred, second stages by the dozen per year and Starlink satellites by the thousand, and makes ~70–80 % of parts in-house (est.), so the factory itself goes down the curve. Reuse cuts how many boosters it builds, but the factory still learns on everything that’s still expendable.
